Lesson Introduction

Your app is built, signed and bundled (lesson 49). Now people have to find it, trust it and install it. That job is done by your store listing: the page on Google Play with your icon, name, screenshots and description. For many apps, the listing is the difference between 50 installs and 50,000.

This lesson is Module 1: Getting started with the basics of the Google Play Store Listing Certificate (https://playacademy.withgoogle.com/certificate), a free, self-paced course from Google Play Academy for developers and marketers. Lessons 51–53 cover the other three modules. Throughout, we teach the skills in our own words and with our own worked examples. We do not reproduce the course text, and we cannot see or share the exam, so always do the official course for the certificate.

Honesty note. Google Play Console changes its menus and wording often, and policy rules move. Where this lesson names a menu or a limit, check the current Play Console Help (https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer) before relying on it. Nobody can promise approval, ranking or install numbers, and neither does this lesson.

By the end you will be able to:

  • Explain what Google Play Console is and who uses it.
  • Create a developer account and understand identity verification, fees and account types.
  • Create an app in the Console and complete the App content declarations.
  • Fill in the main store listing: title, short description, full description, icon, feature graphic, screenshots and video.
  • Meet the baseline requirements for every listing (accuracy, quality, policy).
  • Create your first internal test release and understand the review process.
  • Understand the three kinds of listing assets and the numbers you need.

Big idea: your listing is a promise to the user. Everything in it must be true, clear and quickly understood, because a visitor decides in seconds.

Phase 3 of 11
Conceptual Understanding: What Is Google Play Console?

Google Play Console (https://play.google.com/console) is the website and app where developers publish and manage apps on Google Play. Different people use it in different ways:

Role What they do in Console
Developer Upload bundles, manage releases and testing tracks, fix crashes
Product / marketing Write the listing, run experiments, watch acquisition reports
Designer Supply icons, screenshots and graphics
Support Reply to reviews
Owner / admin Manage users, payments and permissions

The Console has areas such as Dashboard, Statistics, Release (testing and production tracks), Grow users (store listings, experiments, custom listings), Monitor and improve (Android vitals, ratings and reviews) and Policy and programmes (app content, policy status). The names and layout change, but these jobs stay the same.

How a user sees your listing

text
 Google Play search results         Your store listing page
 +-----------------------+         +---------------------------------------------+
 | [icon] App name       |  tap    | [feature graphic or video]                  |
 |  short description    | ----->  | [icon]  App name         [Install]          |
 |  4.5 star  1M+        |         |   Developer name          ratings, downloads|
 +-----------------------+         | [screenshot][screenshot][screenshot] ...    |
                                   | About this app (short + full description)   |
                                   | Data safety | Reviews | Developer contact   |
                                   +---------------------------------------------+

The user first sees only the icon, title and short description in search or on a list. Only a user who is interested continues to the listing page. So the first three items carry the most weight.

Phase 4 of 11
Step-by-Step Build 1: The Developer Account

Account types

When you register, you choose between a personal and an organisation account. The best choice depends on who owns the app.

Personal Organisation
For An individual developer A company, school, club or NGO
Identity check Government ID and a verified payment profile Organisation verification (for example a D-U-N-S number in many countries) plus an ID
Testing requirement before production Currently stricter: a closed test with a minimum number of testers for a minimum period (see lesson 49; check Help for the current rule) Different requirements
Public name shown Your name or developer name Organisation name

For a class project or a first app, personal is fine. For a business such as Techbase Consultant Services, an organisation account is the proper choice because the app is not "owned" by one person's account.

Registration steps (typical)

  1. Sign in to Play Console with a Google account you will keep for years.
  2. Pay the one-time registration fee (at the time of writing a small one-time amount in US dollars; check the current fee and accepted payment methods in your country).
  3. Provide your developer name (shown to users), contact email and phone (verified), and website if you have one.
  4. Complete identity verification with the documents Google asks for. This can take days. Do it early, before you plan to launch.
  5. If you charge for apps or sell in-app items, set up a payments profile; Google needs bank details for payouts. Availability of paid apps and merchant accounts depends on your country.

Security first. Turn on 2-Step Verification for the account, use a password manager, and add teammates as users with limited permissions instead of sharing the owner's password. Never share the account credentials in chat.

Phase 5 of 11
Step-by-Step Build 2: Create the App and Complete the Dashboard
  1. In Play Console choose Create app.
  2. Fill in: - App name (what users see; you can change it later). - Default language of your listing. - App or game. - Free or paid. Warning: a free app cannot be changed to paid later; a paid app can become free. Choose carefully.
  3. Accept the Developer Program Policies and US export laws declarations.
  4. Click Create app.

The Dashboard now shows a setup checklist (names vary: "Set up your app", "Finish setting up your app"). Typical tasks:

Task What you provide
Privacy policy A public URL of your privacy policy page
App access If parts need a login, give reviewers a test account
Ads Whether your app contains ads
Content rating A questionnaire (violence, language, user-generated content...) that produces age ratings
Target audience and content Age groups the app targets. Choosing children triggers additional policy requirements
Data safety A form declaring what data you collect, why, whether it is shared, encrypted, and whether users can delete it
Government apps, financial features, health apps Declarations for sensitive categories where relevant
App category and contact details Category (Education, Tools...), tags, store contact email, website, phone

Privacy policy basics

If your app handles any personal data (and most do, even an email address or an advertising ID), you need a privacy policy at a public URL. It must say what you collect, why, how it is stored and shared, how long you keep it, and how a user can ask for deletion. Free generators exist, but you remain responsible for its accuracy; a policy that says "we collect nothing" while your app uses analytics is a policy violation. Write it from your real data flows.

The Data safety form

Think of this as a nutrition label for data. Walk through your app and libraries: analytics SDKs, crash reporting, ads, login, location, contacts, photos. For each data type declare collected, shared, purpose, and whether collection is optional. Honest, accurate answers matter. Google compares declarations with what it sees, and wrong answers can lead to rejection or removal. If a library collects data, you must declare it.

Phase 6 of 11
Step-by-Step Build 3: The Main Store Listing

In the Console go to the Main store listing page (under Grow users → Store presence or similar; names vary). Fill in each field. We use the running example of an app called Class Register, a free attendance app for Nigerian teachers.

Text fields

Field Limit (check current Help) Purpose
App name (title) 30 characters What the app is, and a key search phrase
Short description 80 characters One-line pitch; shown in search and on the listing
Full description 4,000 characters Features, benefits and detail; searchable

Title. Use your brand name and, if it fits and is truthful, a short descriptor: Class Register: Attendance (26 characters). Policy forbids misleading names, emoji or symbols for decoration, ALL CAPS shouting, and claims such as "No.1", "best", "free" or price/promotion words in the title.

Short description. Lead with the main benefit in plain language:

text
Mark attendance in seconds, even offline, and share class reports.

That is 66 characters. A quick counting function lets you check your own text. It is a tiny program, and a good reminder that characters are not words:

kotlin
fun checkLength(label: String, text: String, limit: Int) {
    val ok = text.length <= limit
    println("$label: ${text.length}/$limit ${if (ok) "OK" else "TOO LONG"}")
}

fun main() {
    checkLength("Title", "Class Register: Attendance", 30)
    checkLength(
        "Short",
        "Mark attendance in seconds, even offline, and share class reports.",
        80
    )
}

Output:
Title: 26/30 OK
Short: 66/80 OK

Full description. Structure it for people who skim:

text
Class Register helps teachers take attendance quickly, even without internet.

WHY TEACHERS LIKE IT
- Tap once per student: present, absent or late
- Works offline; syncs when you are online
- Weekly and termly reports you can share as PDF
- Free; no ads in the attendance screens

HOW IT WORKS
1. Add your classes and students
2. Mark the register each morning
3. Share a report with the head teacher or parents

DESIGNED FOR REAL CLASSROOMS
Large buttons, works on low-storage phones, supports large text and TalkBack.

Questions? support@yourdomain.ng

Principles for the full description:

  1. First three lines matter most; they show before "Read more".
  2. Say what the app does in plain words, then what benefit the user gets, then details.
  3. Use short paragraphs and lists.
  4. Include natural keywords a user would search for ("attendance", "register", "school"). Do not keyword-stuff: repeating words, listing unrelated apps or competitors' names breaks policy and reads badly.
  5. Be accurate: do not describe features that do not exist, and do not claim endorsements or awards you do not have.
  6. Mention important requirements (internet, account, in-app purchases).
  7. Provide contact details.

Graphic assets

Always check the current numbers in the Console's upload dialogs (they list size and format requirements). Typical values at the time of writing:

Asset Typical requirement Notes
App icon 512 × 512 px, 32-bit PNG, up to about 1 MB Do not add rounded corners or shadows yourself; Google Play applies the mask
Feature graphic 1024 × 500 px, JPEG or 24-bit PNG (no alpha) Shown at the top of the listing and in some promotions
Phone screenshots At least 2 (up to 8); JPEG or PNG; each side between about 320 and 3,840 px; the long side no more than twice the short side Show real UI
Tablet screenshots Optional but recommended if the app supports tablets (7-inch and 10-inch separately) Needed for good tablet and large-screen presentation
Promo video A YouTube URL Public or unlisted, not age-restricted, no ads, monetisation off
Wear OS, TV, Automotive, Chromebook screenshots Where you support them

A helpful tiny script checks screenshot sizes before you upload (it needs the Pillow library, installed with pip install pillow):

python
from pathlib import Path
from PIL import Image

for path in sorted(Path("screenshots").glob("*.png")):
    w, h = Image.open(path).size
    long_side, short_side = max(w, h), min(w, h)
    ok = 320 <= short_side and long_side <= 3840 and long_side <= 2 * short_side
    print(f"{path.name}: {w}x{h} {'OK' if ok else 'CHECK'}")

Localisation

You can add translated listings. Even a short, accurate translation into the languages of your market (Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, French or Arabic, as appropriate) can help discovery and trust. Use professional or native-speaker review; poor machine translation harms trust. Lesson 52 covers this in depth.

Step-by-Step Build 4: Baseline Requirements for Every Listing

Every listing is checked against Google Play policies. The principles are not complicated, and breaking them is the most common reason for rejection.

Accurate and not misleading

  • The title, icon and screenshots must represent the app truthfully. Screenshots must show real in-app UI, not a mock-up of features that do not exist.
  • No fake ratings, reviews or install counts, no text in the icon or title referring to rankings or promotions ("Top", "#1", "50% off").
  • No claims of medical, financial or legal benefits you cannot substantiate.

Original and respectful of others' rights

  • Do not copy another developer's name, icon or branding, or impersonate a well-known organisation.
  • Do not use copyrighted images, music or text without a licence. (The stock photos, fonts and icons you use must allow commercial use.)

Appropriate

  • Content must match the rating you declared.
  • Policies cover user-generated content, ads, permissions, spam and minimum functionality, deceptive behaviour and families. A basic app that simply repackages a website or shows nothing useful may be rejected under minimum-functionality rules.

Safe

  • Declare data practices truthfully (data safety form).
  • Request only needed permissions; sensitive permissions get extra review.
  • Include a visible way for users to delete their account and data if your app lets them create an account (Play requires this for apps that allow account creation).

Quality

  • Fix crashes before publishing (lesson 49 pre-launch report). Google measures Android vitals (crash and ANR rates) and may reduce the visibility of apps with very bad ones.
  • Use targetSdk at the current requirement.

Treat the policies as a checklist to run before each submission. Reading the Developer Program Policy Center pages once will save weeks of rejections.

Phase 8 of 11
Step-by-Step Build 5: First Release and Review
  1. Release → Testing → Internal testing, create a new release.
  2. Upload the AAB (lesson 49). Play checks the signature, version code, target API, permissions and policy issues.
  3. Enter release notes (what changed). Notes are per language.
  4. Add testers (an email list) and share the opt-in link they receive.
  5. Save, review and roll out. Internal tests are usually available within minutes.
  6. When you are ready for wider testing, repeat in closed testing, and finally Production, where review typically takes from a few hours to several days. New developers and sensitive apps can wait longer. Plan buffer time before a launch date or an exam week.

What happens after you submit

text
 Upload bundle  ->  automated checks  ->  policy review (automated + human)
        |                                        |
   errors shown                         approved: goes live (or on your schedule)
   (fix, re-upload)                     rejected: email + Policy status page
                                         with the policy and what to change

If the app is rejected, read the exact reason on Policy status, fix all the problems mentioned (not only the first), and resubmit or appeal with a clear, respectful explanation. Repeated violations can put the whole developer account at risk, so do not "try again and see".

Managed publishing

You can turn on managed publishing so approved changes do not go live until you press Publish. This is useful for launching on a chosen day, or for releasing a code change together with new store text.

Phase 10 of 11
Common Beginner Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

Mistake #1: Starting registration the week before launch. Symptom: verification delays. Fix: register early.

Mistake #2: Choosing "Free" without thinking. Symptom: cannot charge later. Fix: use in-app purchases or a second paid app if you might charge.

Mistake #3: Stuffing the title with keywords. Symptom: rejection or lower trust. Fix: brand plus a short truthful descriptor.

Mistake #4: Screenshots that show a mock-up, a device frame with fake features or tiny text. Fix: real UI, short captions, readable text.

Mistake #5: Copy-pasting a generic privacy policy. Symptom: mismatch with real data practices. Fix: write it from your actual data flows.

Mistake #6: Ignoring Data safety. Symptom: update blocked or app removed. Fix: audit every SDK.

Mistake #7: Using copyrighted images, music or competitors' logos. Fix: own or licensed assets only.

Mistake #8: Not testing the app access flow. Symptom: reviewer cannot log in, rejection. Fix: provide a working demo account.

Mistake #9: Fixing only one of several policy issues. Fix: read the whole message and fix everything.

Mistake #10: Promising results. Symptom: disappointed clients. Fix: never promise rankings or installs.

Phase 11 of 11
Knowledge Check

Question 1. What are the three listing items users see first in search results?

The icon, the app title and the short description (plus rating information).

Question 2. Why must a free app be chosen carefully?

A free app cannot be changed to a paid app later (a paid app can become free), so if you may want to charge, plan in-app purchases or another paid app.

Question 3. What must screenshots show?

The real app interface and features users actually get, not fake or unavailable features, and no misleading content.

Question 4. What is the Data safety form for?

To declare honestly what user data the app and its libraries collect or share, why, and whether users can control or delete it. Inaccurate declarations can lead to rejection or removal.

Question 5. Where do you send your first build for testing before production?

To the internal testing track (then closed or open testing as needed), and only later to production, ideally with a staged rollout.

Complete Store Listing Kit

Project goal: Produce a complete, reviewable store listing kit for an app of your choice (your own or Class Register), as a folder of files you could paste into Play Console.

Requirements:

  1. listing/en/title.txt (30 chars max), short.txt (80 chars max) and full.txt (at most 4,000 characters, with the structure from this lesson).
  2. A check_listing.py script that validates the limits and prints OK/TOO LONG for each file.
  3. A privacy-policy.md outline matching your real data flows.
  4. A Data safety worksheet (table of each data type, whether collected, shared, purpose, optional).
  5. An assets list with the size and format for the icon, feature graphic and at least four phone screenshots; screenshots exist as files.
  6. A policy self-check with at least ten yes/no questions answered.

Stretch goal: write a second language version of the three text files and have a native speaker review it.

check_listing.py:

check_listing.py
from pathlib import Path

LIMITS = {"title.txt": 30, "short.txt": 80, "full.txt": 4000}
base = Path("listing/en")

for name, limit in LIMITS.items():
    text = (base / name).read_text(encoding="utf-8").strip()
    status = "OK" if len(text) <= limit else "TOO LONG"
    print(f"{name}: {len(text)}/{limit} {status}")

full = (base / "full.txt").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
first_lines = "\n".join(full.splitlines()[:3])
print("\nFirst three lines (shown before 'Read more'):\n" + first_lines)
